What is simplified? The three “photographs” are synthetic images generated from a hidden reference scene so that the target is known. The 3D locations shown as COLMAP points are illustrative sparse correspondences, not the output of a real SfM run. The toy renderer uses spherical Gaussian footprints, front-to-back alpha compositing, fixed RGB colors, and finite-difference gradients. Real 3DGS uses anisotropic 3D Gaussians, projected covariance, spherical harmonics, differentiable GPU rasterization, and adaptive densification/pruning. Nevertheless, the core learning loop and the separation between stored parameters, predicted image, and target image are the same.

Detailed explanations: follow the full pipeline

The simulation above shows what you can click. The sections below explain what we start with, why each step is needed, what the figure shows, and what you should remember. Each stage connects to the previous and next one.

How to read this tutorial: You already know ML concepts such as loss, gradients, and optimization. Treat the reference image as a supervised learning target; the rendered image as a prediction; and the Gaussians as the stored trainable representation that gets updated. The toy has five Gaussians, unlike a real 3DGS scene with many more primitives.

Pipeline stage 1 of 5

1. Input photographs

What are we starting with?

We begin with several photographs of one physical scene taken from different camera positions and viewing angles. Imagine walking around a chair and photographing it from the front, side, and back. The chair is the same object in every image, but its 2D appearance and pixel coordinates change with the viewpoint.

Why is this step needed?

A photograph is a 2D projection: by itself, it generally cannot determine the entire 3D shape or the distance to every visible surface. When an object or distinctive feature appears in overlapping images, its changing position across those views provides constraints on 3D geometry. This is the information the next step (Structure from Motion / COLMAP) uses to estimate camera poses and sparse points. These original images will also be used much later as fixed training targets.

What is happening in the figure?

  • Camera A observes the scene from one viewpoint.
  • Camera B observes the same scene from a different viewpoint.
  • Camera C provides a third overlapping observation.

The three panels labeled Original image are fixed RGB targets. In this demonstration they are synthetic images created from a hidden reference scene. In a real 3DGS experiment, they would be your captured photographs. Click cameras A, B, and C and notice that the reference view changes, but the underlying scene is shared.

Key takeaway: Multiple 2D images are observations of the same 3D world; their overlap supports reconstruction, and their RGB values later supervise rendering. The original photos do not change during training.

Pipeline stage 2 of 5

2. COLMAP: sparse 3D reconstruction

What are we starting with?

We have overlapping photographs, but we do not yet know reliable 3D coordinates for the visible scene points or the cameras' positions and orientations in a shared coordinate system.

Why is this step needed?

3DGS needs camera parameters to render each training view, and standard vanilla 3DGS needs initial 3D points to place its first Gaussians. COLMAP provides both. Once these sparse geometric estimates are available, the next step can initialize one Gaussian center at each reconstructed point.

What is happening in the figure?

A typical Structure-from-Motion reconstruction detects local image features, matches those features across overlapping photographs, estimates camera geometry, triangulates corresponding observations into 3D points, and refines the estimates (typically using bundle adjustment). COLMAP outputs registered camera poses, camera calibration information, and a sparse point cloud. Sparse means it reconstructs selected reliable points, not necessarily every visible pixel.

In the left-hand drawing, small point markers schematically represent the reconstructed 3D points, while A/B/C represent camera viewpoints. This tutorial does not actually execute COLMAP; the points and cameras are a conceptual illustration of its output.

Key takeaway: COLMAP does not simply turn every 2D pixel into a 3D point. It estimates 3D locations from cross-view correspondences. Those sparse positions are geometric seeds, not trained Gaussian primitives yet.

Pipeline stage 3 of 5

3. Initialize 3D Gaussians

What are we starting with?

We now have a sparse 3D point cloud and estimated camera parameters. A reconstructed point gives a 3D location (and often an RGB estimate), but a point by itself has no spatial extent or orientation.

Why is this step needed?

The Gaussian Splatting renderer operates on spatial primitives, not on unextended geometric points. Each initial point seeds a Gaussian with a center, spatial spread, orientation, opacity, and appearance parameters. This turns the rough COLMAP reconstruction into a trainable scene representation that can be rendered and improved in Steps 4 and 5.

What is happening in the figure?

Choose a Gaussian using the selector. Its stored scale controls the extent of its rendered influence, and its stored opacity controls how strongly it contributes; try changing both sliders. The illustrated center stays at its 3D position when only these sliders change.

In the official vanilla 3DGS initialization, positions come from COLMAP; initial color comes from the point RGB; scale is estimated from nearby points; rotation starts at identity; opacity is given an initial value; higher-order spherical-harmonic coefficients begin at zero. In this simplified demo, each Gaussian has a position, a single spherical scale, opacity, and a fixed RGB color. It does not model rotation or train spherical-harmonic coefficients.

Key takeaway: The initial Gaussian is not an empty shell waiting to be painted. It already has numerical parameters that can generate an image. However, those parameters are only rough starting estimates and are not yet optimized for the reference photographs.

Pipeline stage 4 of 5

4. Rendering and reconstruction loss

What are we starting with?

We have a collection of initialized 3D Gaussians and calibrated camera views, but no guarantee that the current representation reproduces the original photographs.

Why is this step needed?

To improve the Gaussians, training first needs a prediction and a measure of how wrong it is. Rendering supplies the predicted 2D image for a chosen camera; comparison with that camera's unchanged photograph supplies the reconstruction loss that drives Step 5.

What is happening in the figure?

Select Camera A, B, or C and compare the three panels:
  • Original image: the fixed 2D reference (training target).
  • Rendered image: the current model's prediction, computed from the Gaussian parameters at the selected camera pose.
  • Difference: a display of pixel-wise disagreement; brighter regions indicate larger errors.

To render, the system projects Gaussians into the image plane, determines which projected footprints contribute to pixels, and combines their RGB contributions using opacity and visibility ordering. The numeric loss shown here is mean absolute RGB error, normalized by 255. The official 3DGS loss also includes a structural similarity term; this toy deliberately uses only L1-type error.

Key takeaway: Rendering is a forward computation: current 3D parameters plus camera viewpoint produce pixel predictions. It does not paint new information onto the Gaussian or update the model. The loss compares those predicted pixels to observed ones.

Pipeline stage 5 of 5

5. Training and parameter updates

What are we starting with?

We have the same shared Gaussian model, reference images, camera poses, and an image reconstruction loss. The model starts imperfectly and should improve through repeated optimization.

Why is this step needed?

The initial Gaussian positions, scales, and opacities do not yet explain all camera observations. After Step 4 measures the image error, training updates the Gaussian parameters so that subsequent rendered images fit the photographs better. All cameras supervise one shared 3D representation, rather than separate models for A, B, and C.

What is happening in the figure?

Press Train 1 step (current camera) to update the model using only the selected viewpoint, or Train 15 steps (A → B → C) to use all three views across consecutive steps. Each training step follows this loop:
  1. Select a camera and its fixed reference image.
  2. Render the current Gaussians into a predicted image.
  3. Compute the image reconstruction loss.
  4. Estimate gradients of loss with respect to the Gaussian parameters.
  5. Apply an optimizer update, then repeat.

Watch the rendered image, the current-view loss, and the overall loss history. After training from Camera A, switch to B: Camera B's prediction may also change, because the Gaussians are shared. In this demo, the optimizer adjusts 3D positions, scalar scale, and opacity via finite-difference gradients and an Adam-style update. It does not optimize RGB, rotations, SH, or Gaussian count.

In real vanilla 3DGS, backpropagation through a differentiable rasterizer updates geometric and appearance parameters; densification and pruning can also add or remove Gaussians during training. Loss need not decrease on every individual step for every camera.

Key takeaway: Learnable parameters change; training photographs do not. Rendering computes predictions, the loss quantifies disagreement, gradients determine update directions, and the optimizer modifies the shared 3D model. Over many steps and views, the learned representation becomes more faithful.
